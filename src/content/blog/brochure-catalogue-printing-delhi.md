A brochure often does its work when you are not in the room. It sits on a dealer's desk in Karol Bagh, goes home in a customer's bag from a showroom in Lajpat Nagar, or gets passed around a purchase team in Gurgaon. So it needs to be easy to read, sturdy enough to survive being handled, and clear about what you want the reader to do next.

Planning it well comes down to four choices: format and page count, paper, binding or folding, and finish.

## Start with what the reader needs

Before you think about paper, list what the brochure must contain. An introduction, a list of services, product photos, a price list, a contact page. This list decides the format.

- **A few key messages**: a single sheet with a fold is enough.
- **A company profile with some photos**: a small stapled booklet works well.
- **A full product range with codes and specifications**: you need a bound catalogue.

One tip: keep anything that changes often, such as prices or offers, out of the main brochure. Put it on a separate insert sheet. That way the expensive printed piece stays useful for longer.

## Folded leaflets and the common folds

A single sheet can be folded in several ways, each giving a different reading order.

**Half fold (bi-fold)** gives four panels. Simple and clean, good for menus, event programmes and short company profiles.

**Tri-fold (letter fold)** gives six panels and fits neatly in an envelope or a display rack. This is the classic clinic, school and service brochure.

**Z-fold** opens like a zigzag. It suits step-by-step content or a map.

**Gate fold** has two side panels that open like doors to reveal a wide centre. It is good for a product launch or a single big image.

With folds, the panels are not all the same width. The inside panel is slightly narrower so it can tuck in. Use the correct template from your printer, otherwise text ends up on the fold.

## Page count and binding

For booklets and catalogues, the binding method affects the page count, the look and how the book opens.

**Centre stapling (saddle stitch)** is the most common and economical option. Sheets are folded and stapled through the spine. Pages must be in multiples of four. It works best for thinner booklets. Very thick ones do not close flat and the inner pages stick out at the edge.

**Perfect binding** glues the pages into a square spine, like a paperback book. It looks premium, holds many pages and lets you print the title on the spine so it can stand on a shelf. It does not open completely flat, so keep important content away from the inner margin.

**Wiro or spiral binding** uses a metal or plastic coil. The book opens fully flat and can fold back on itself. It is practical for price books, menus and technical catalogues that sit on a counter.

**Hard binding** is for annual reports, coffee-table books and special editions. It takes longer and is usually done in smaller quantities.

## Paper and GSM

Inside pages and covers are usually on different papers.

- **Inside pages**: art paper in the mid range of GSM is common. Gloss art paper makes photos look rich. Matt art paper is easier to read and looks more refined.
- **Cover**: a heavier board, often laminated, so the brochure does not curl or get dog-eared.
- **Folded leaflets**: a medium to heavier paper so the folds stay crisp. Very thick paper can crack along the fold, so ask for creasing before folding.

If you are printing in large quantities for distribution at events or with newspapers, a lighter paper keeps the cost and weight sensible. For a catalogue that dealers will use for a year, choose a heavier paper and a laminated cover.

## Finishes that are worth it

- **Matt or gloss lamination on the cover** protects it from handling and spills
- **Velvet lamination** gives a soft, premium cover feel
- **Spot UV on the logo or title** adds a subtle shine
- **Die-cut shapes or pockets**, useful for folders that hold loose sheets and visiting cards

Use finishes on the cover. The inside pages rarely need them.

## Offset or digital printing

For short runs, such as a few dozen copies for a meeting or a sample batch for approval, digital printing is quick and practical. For large quantities, offset printing gives a lower cost per copy and very consistent colour across the run. Ask your printer which method they plan to use for your quantity, and why.

## Before you send it to print

1. Proofread everything, especially phone numbers, product codes and spellings of place names.
2. Use high-resolution photos. Images taken from a website or WhatsApp usually look soft in print.
3. Ask for a printed proof or a dummy copy, so you can feel the paper and check the page order.
4. Agree on delivery: bundled, boxed, or split between your offices in Noida and Faridabad.
