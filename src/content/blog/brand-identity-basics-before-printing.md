Many businesses discover they have a branding problem only when they place their first big print order. The signmaker asks for a vector logo and all anyone can find is a WhatsApp image. The visiting cards are a different blue from the shop board. The brochure uses a font that nobody can name.

None of this is hard to fix, but it is much easier to fix once, at the start, than on every job. This post covers the basics: logo files, colours, fonts and a simple brand guide.

## Logo files

Your logo is used on everything from a visiting card to a hoarding. It needs to be available in the right formats.

**Vector files** are the master copy. They store the logo as shapes, so they can be scaled to any size without losing sharpness. Ask your designer for:

- AI (Adobe Illustrator) or CDR (CorelDRAW)
- A vector PDF or EPS
- An SVG for the website

**Image files** like PNG and JPG are fine for screens, emails and social media, but they go blurry when enlarged. A PNG with a transparent background is useful for presentations.

You also need a few versions of the logo:

- **Full colour** on a white background
- **Reverse or white** for dark backgrounds
- **Single colour** (black, or one brand colour) for engraving, embroidery and stamps
- **Horizontal and stacked** layouts, if your logo has a name and a symbol

Single-colour versions matter more than people think. Laser engraving on a bottle, embroidery on a polo and frosting on glass all need a simple one-colour logo.

## Brand colours

"Our blue" is not a colour specification. Every brand colour should have codes for each place it will be used:

- **CMYK** for printing on paper, flex and vinyl
- **Pantone (PMS)** for exact matches in offset printing, packaging and some merchandise
- **RGB and HEX** for websites, screens and social media

Screens and printers work differently, so the same colour can look brighter on a phone than on paper. Fixing these codes once, and checking a printed sample, avoids the most common complaint in printing: the colour does not match.

Keep the palette small. One or two main colours and a couple of supporting colours are usually enough.

## Fonts

Pick one or two fonts and stick to them:

- A **heading font** for titles, signage and big text
- A **body font** for brochures, letters and websites

Check the licence of each font, especially if it will be used on a website or in signage. Keep the font files with your logo files, so any designer or printer can use them. When sending print files, convert text to outlines or embed the fonts in the PDF, so nothing changes at the printer's end.

## A simple brand guide

A brand guide does not need to be a thick book. For most small and medium businesses in Delhi NCR, one to four pages are enough. Include:

1. **Logo versions** and which one to use where
2. **Clear space**: how much empty space to leave around the logo
3. **Minimum size**: the smallest the logo should appear
4. **Colour codes**: CMYK, Pantone, RGB and HEX for each colour
5. **Fonts**: names and where each is used
6. **Do's and don'ts**: do not stretch the logo, do not change its colours, do not place it on busy photos

Share this guide as a PDF with every vendor. A signmaker in Gurgaon, a packaging printer in Noida and a web designer working remotely will all produce work that looks like it came from the same company.

## Organise the files

Make one shared folder with:

- Logo files in all formats and versions
- Font files
- The brand guide PDF
- A few approved sample designs (visiting card, letterhead, social post)

Give it a clear name and share the link whenever anyone asks. This one folder will save hours over the next few years.

## When to do this

The best time is before your first major print order: shop signage, office branding, a product launch or an exhibition. If you already have printed material in use, it is still worth doing now, so the next reprint is cleaner.
